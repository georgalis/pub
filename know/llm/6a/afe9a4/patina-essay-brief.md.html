Prose From Somewhere

A short introduction to patina

In Brief

Writing made by a machine tends to sound as if it comes from nowhere. It is smooth and hard to place, and readers have learned that sound. Once they hear it, they trust the writing less. Patina is a writing skill that answers this. It gives prose the sound of somewhere---a trade, a region, an era---so that readers hear a person who belongs to a world instead of a machine that belongs to none. Every piece of writing sounds like it comes from somewhere. Patina lets the writer choose where.

The Trust a Reader Gives

Readers extend a quiet kind of trust at the first line. They assume the writer knows the world being described and speaks the way people in that world speak. Call this membership warrant: the reader's sense that the writer belongs. Membership warrant is never asked for and seldom noticed. It shows itself only when it is withdrawn, and it is withdrawn all at once. A single phrase the reader has seen too often in machine writing can send them back over every page, hearing each one again with less trust than before.

Where the Name Comes From

A new bronze casting leaves the foundry bright and even, the same color everywhere. Over many years, weather and hands give it a patina, a surface of greens and browns that makes it look as if it has always stood where it stands. Founders do not wait for time. They warm the metal with a torch, brush on a chemical wash, and seal the color under wax, laying on in an afternoon what the weather would lay on over decades. No one who buys the bronze is fooled, and no one is meant to be. The patina is how the work is finished. The skill takes its name from that practice, and it finishes prose the way a founder finishes bronze.

What the Skill Does

Patina works in two directions at once. It removes the phrases readers now link with machine writing, and it adds forms that a real community already uses: its words and sayings, its habits of phrasing, its familiar comparisons and references. The skill calls such a community a parlance, meaning the shared speech of a trade or a place. Patina invents none of these forms. Their value is that the reader has heard them before, in other voices, and knows where those voices come from. The skill keeps them in a library of simple files, one for each parlance, and checks how long each form has been in use against Google's record of English books, the Ngram data[^1]. Forms with some age on them are preferred, since they carry the most of the place they come from.

Comparisons, Chosen With Care

The skill also reaches for figures of speech, the comparisons and references that carry an unfamiliar subject over to something the reader already knows. Readers forgive a comparison that fits loosely. What they will not forgive is one in the wrong mood, because they carry the mood across whole. A church bell shows the difference. The same bronze is tolled slowly for a funeral and rung in a quick peal for a wedding, and the town knows which it is hearing before anyone says a word. So patina settles the mood first, which it calls the cast, and admits only the comparisons that sound in that mood.

Choosing Where

The machine will go on writing from nowhere unless someone chooses otherwise. Every piece of writing sounds like it comes from somewhere, and a reader will place it whether the writer chose the place or not. Patina is offered as a way to make that choice on purpose: to finish the prose, as a founder finishes bronze, so that it rings true to the reader who hears it.
