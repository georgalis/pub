Patina

Prose that belongs somewhere, and an instrument for putting it there

The Recognition

A reader is three paragraphs into a document that has given no offense: the sentences are clean, and the argument moves at the level pace of something that has never once been tired. Then a phrase arrives that the reader has met too often and always in the same company---a rich tapestry, a pivotal role, a promise to delve---and the reading stops. Nothing false has been said. Something has been heard. The reader turns back through the pages already read and hears them again, and every line that had seemed only smooth now rings of the mold that cast it.

The recognition runs backward, and that is its cost. Nothing in the document changed between the first page and the third; the reader changed, and what the reader took back was a credit older and quieter than agreement, given at the first line without being asked: the working belief that a person stood behind the prose and belonged to the world the prose described. A 2024 study of scientific abstracts measured the ground this belief now stands on, finding that certain words surged in frequency after language models came into wide use in late 2022, with delve chief among them[^1]. Readers learn the machine's parlance as they learn any parlance, by its emblems, and once they hear one they place the speaker. The prose had seemed to come from nowhere, and nowhere, it turns out, is a place with a speech of its own.

No prose is unaffiliated. A text that sounds like no community sounds, to a reader who has learned the machine's habits, like the machine, and the instrument this essay introduces begins from that fact. Its name is patina, and its work is to choose the affiliation.

The Credit a Reader Extends

The credit withdrawn in that third paragraph deserves a name, since everything else depends on it: call it membership warrant, the reader's unreflective inference that the author belongs. Membership warrant is not argued into being. Aristotle's Rhetoric holds that the credibility of character should arise from the speech itself, not from anything the audience believed about the speaker beforehand[^2], and membership warrant is that credibility in its plainest form, conferred sentence by sentence as the reader hears the forms of a trade, a region, or an era used as their users use them. It is extended at reading speed and withdrawn at the same speed, and when it goes it takes the pages already read along with it.

The old test of a Londoner worked on the same principle. A Cockney, by the traditional reckoning, was a child born within the sound of Bow bells, the peal of St Mary-le-Bow, so that belonging was measured by how far a bronze note could carry across the roofs. Prose has its bells: the words of a trade ring out to the bounds of its parish, and a reader who hears them knows where the writer was raised. The counting house kept a harder form of the test. A true silver coin dropped on the board rang, while a counterfeit poured in base metal and washed to look like silver fell with a dull and leaden note, and ring true is what our speech has kept of that. Synthetic prose is poured in a fine alloy. It still does not ring.

The Founders' Answer

A bronze leaves the chaser's bench bright and uniform, every surface the same raw gold, the seams filed down and the channels of the pour cut away, and in that state it looks like what it is, a thing made yesterday. Weather would give it a skin in time, green where the rain runs and dark in the hollows where the dust settles and stays, until after a lifetime of use it looks as though it has always stood where it stands. Founders do not wait for that. They lay the torch to the metal until it will take a stain, brush on the wash---liver of sulfur for the browns and blacks, ferric nitrate for the reds, cupric nitrate for the blues and greens---and watch the color bloom and blush across the bronze as the liquid boils off, then seal what the flame has fixed beneath a coat of wax. The practice is called patination, and no one who buys a cast bronze believes it stood a century in the rain; they believe it is finished.

The machine is a competent founder. It pours clean, seldom leaves a void, and brings the casting out of the investment---the mold broken away once the metal sets---sound and complete, with the sprues still standing where the metal ran in and the whole surface one flat color. What it will not do unasked is chase the casting or color it. Patina does both, filing off the marks of the pour and laying on the color of a place, and it takes its name from the second because the second is what a reader sees. What time deposits on metal over decades, patination deposits in an afternoon, and the eye that admires the one is not deceived by the other, since both are true to the bronze.

Two Motions in One Pour

The instrument does its chasing and its coloring as one motion. Displacement removes the markers of the parlance the prose is leaving, the machine's first among them, and affiliation lays on the markers of the parlance it is joining; neither ranks above the other, since the prose is only moving from one parish to the next. A parlance, in the instrument's accounting, is any community whose speech a reader can place---a trade, a region, an era, an institution, a stance---and what the instrument lays on is only what that community already holds: its words and idioms, its habits of construction, its spellings, its figures, its allusions. Patina mints nothing. Its whole value is that the reader has heard these forms before, in other mouths, and knows whose mouths they were.

Two biases steer the choosing. The first leans toward emblems, the forms a reader at large recognizes, over the operative forms only members notice, because the aim is an affiliation within the reader's reach rather than a disguise fit to pass an insider's audit; the author may press that lean as far as caricature, which the instrument counts a portrait, drawn on purpose, of the parlance it exaggerates. The second leans toward forms steeped in time, from the antique through the longstanding to the newly settled, and it takes the measure of that time from the English print record, through Google Books Ngram data kept in a library of files, one to a parlance. The library outranks the model's own sense of what is current, and for good reason, since that sense is the very thing under correction. Its evidence works as the hallmark worked at Goldsmiths' Hall, where the assay was made once, by someone other than the seller, and the mark travelled with the metal ever after.

The Weight a Figure Carries

The instrument's strongest device is the figure that carries a subject across to something the reader already holds. Richards's Philosophy of Rhetoric named its parts in 1936---the tenor carried, the vehicle that carries it, and the ground they share[^3]---and patina adds a working law to that anatomy: the mapping is never one to one, but the association always is. Readers know an analogy is partial and forgive a thin ground. What they carry over whole is the vehicle's standing, its familiarity and the light it stands in, so that a subject borne by an accepted vehicle arrives already half accepted. It is easier for a reader to take the unfamiliar through the familiar than for a writer to justify the unfamiliar outright, and here lies the chief return on synthesis: the bridges no source supplied.

Everything therefore turns on that light, which the instrument calls the cast and fixes before any figure is chosen. The old rhetoric knew it as decorum, the fitness of manner to matter, and knew as well that decorum fails worst where the fit is nearly right. An exact analogy in the wrong cast carries its subject into a light it should not be received in, however sound its ground, while a marginal one in the right cast does honest work. So the instrument admits no vehicle out of cast at any setting and prefers more vehicles wherever they align, each one a member in the truss that bears the subject over; at its loosest license it will invent vehicles and allusions of its own, and it reports each invention so the author can prove it before the reader does. A figure borrowed from the wrong parish carries the subject there.

Within the Sound of the Bell

Return, then, to the reader three paragraphs in, and let the phrase that arrives there be one the reader has also met before, in the mouths of founders or of the old rhetoricians, used as those users use it. The reading does not stop. The reader hears a sound that belongs somewhere, and the credit goes out again, forward and back, across the pages already read: membership warrant returns the way it left, at reading speed, and brings the whole document with it. Nothing in the argument has changed. The document has only been chased and colored, the marks of the pour filed away and the color of a place laid on.

A church bell is the one casting that answers all of this at once. It is founded in bronze and poured in a single heat; the weather gives its shoulder a patina that tells the eye how long it has hung in its tower; and its note, carried across the roofs, tells the ear which parish it belongs to and who was born within the sound of it. Surface and sound, age and belonging, stand together in one metal. No prose is unaffiliated, and so the writer has only ever been choosing between parishes; the machine's is one parish among many, and the one readers now hear soonest. Patina is offered as the means of choosing another, and of hanging the bell where the reader already listens.
